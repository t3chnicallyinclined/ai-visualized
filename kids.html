How does an AI write a story?

Let's look inside a real one while it writes, one word at a time. You will see that it is numbers and a dice roll, made by people. No magic, no secret mind.

This AI is a small real model called TinyStories (15 million numbers, trained only on simple children's stories). It runs right here in your web browser: nothing you type is sent anywhere. The first visit downloads its numbers (30 MB). Grown-ups and teachers: the full version shows every detail.

Downloading the model's numbers…

Write the start of a story. The AI will add the next words.

1

Chop it into pieces

The AI can't read letters. First it chops your words into pieces it knows, called tokens. Each piece has a number, like a page number in a giant dictionary of 32,000 pieces. (The dot means "starts with a space".)

2

Turn each piece into a list of numbers

Each piece looks up its own list of 288 numbers, which the computer learned before. Here are the first 32 for each piece, as colours (blue = below zero, orange = above). Pieces used in similar ways get similar lists.

3

Down through 6 tunnels of knobs

Now the lists go down through 6 tunnels. Each tunnel is full of knobs (the little dials): 15 million of them in all, set while the AI was being made, and they never move while it writes. In each tunnel the numbers get multiplied by the knobs and mixed with the other pieces. After each tunnel we can peek at what word the numbers point to now.

The ants are pretend: they are just a picture of the work. The knobs' positions and the guesses in the bubbles are the AI's real numbers.

4

Score every word, then roll the dice

At the bottom, the AI gives every one of its 32,000 pieces a score. Here are the top five as chances. Then it rolls a dice with those chances to pick one. That's why it doesn't always pick the top word!

🎲

"Careful" makes the top word win almost every time. "Silly" lets surprising words win more often.

5

Again, and again, and again

The picked word is added to the story, and the whole thing runs again for the next word. That's all it does: one word at a time.

Is it alive? Let's test it!

Press each button: the real AI on this page does the test.

Does it think like a person?No. It's arithmetic.It has 15,191,712 knobs. Making each word means multiplying the numbers by millions of knobs and adding them up. No feelings, no wishes, no secret plan.
Does it choose on its own?No. Same start + careful dice = same story.Set to "careful", it is a machine: the same start gives the same words, every time.
Does it know what's true?No. It writes what sounds right.It learned from stories, so it writes something story-like, true or not. Here it finishes a science fact.
Can it do things by itself, or get loose?No. It only makes the next word when a program asks.On this page it can't click, browse, remember you or send anything. Close the tab and it stops. "AI agents" are programs that people build around a model, and people choose what those programs may do.
Who made it?People.People wrote the stories it learned from, built the computers, wrote the program that turned the knobs during training, and decided when to stop. Every knob is just a number that training set.

So what is actually risky?

The AI is not a monster. The real risks are about how people use it and how much we trust it:

It can sound sure and still be wrong

It always writes smoothly, even when it is making things up (you just saw it with the sun). Check important facts in a book, a trusted website, or with a teacher.

It repeats what it read, fair or unfair

It copies patterns from the text people wrote, including mistakes and unfair ideas about people. Big AIs are trained on huge piles of internet text, so this matters.

People can use it to make fakes

Some people use AI to make fake messages, pictures or voices to trick others. If something online seems surprising or asks for something, stop and check with a grown-up.

Don't share private things

Many AI apps send what you type to a company's computers (this page doesn't). Never type your address, passwords or secrets into an AI app.

It's a tool, not a friend or a teacher

It can help you practise and explore, but it doesn't understand you or care about you. For real help, talk to people you trust.

For teachers and parents

Everything on this page comes from a real model, TinyStories 15M (trained by Andrej Karpathy on the TinyStories dataset of simple stories), running in the browser. The ants are the only pretend part. Ideas by grade:

  • K–2: read steps 1, 4 and 5 aloud. Play "guess the next word" as a class, then roll the dice. Main idea: it picks one word at a time, like a game.
  • 3–5: all five steps, then the "Is it alive?" tests. Try careful vs silly. Main idea: it's numbers made by people, and it can be wrong.
  • 6–8: add the risks section: why fluent isn't the same as true, where unfairness comes from (the training text), and private information.
  • 9–12: move to the full version: tokens, embeddings, attention, the logit lens, the training checkpoints of a real model, and how different model designs compare.
